# Sequence Mode: Evidence Snapshot

This summary distils findings on how to help learners acquire ordered sequences (procedural or declarative) over the short-medium term. Use it to justify design choices in Sequence mode and to calibrate defaults.

## Retrieval Practice (Testing Effect)
- Repeated retrieval drives stronger, longer-lasting sequence memory than additional study alone, even when initial tests are challenging (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006; Karpicke & Roediger, 2008).
- Benefits extend to ordered recall: retrieving upcoming steps strengthens stepwise associations and reduces position errors.
- Practical takeaways: include frequent prompts to produce the next step; recycle missed steps quickly; surface cumulative retrieval rather than only restudying the full list.

## Spacing (Distributed Practice)
- Spacing practice over time reliably improves retention versus massing, across verbal and skill materials (Cepeda et al., 2006; Latimier et al., 2021).
- Spacing interacts with retrieval: slightly effortful, delayed retrieval boosts consolidation more than immediate restudy.
- Practical takeaways: stagger repetitions of each step/transition; avoid drilling a single sequence to fluency in one block; widen gaps after correct recalls, shrink after errors.

## Interleaving / Contextual Interference
- Mixing different sequences or variants during practice (rather than blocked rehearsal) can enhance transfer and adaptable performance, even if it feels harder during practice (Shea & Morgan, 1979; Dahms et al., 2019).
- Works for motor and procedural chains by forcing discriminations between similar steps and by strengthening retrieval cues.
- Practical takeaways: interleave segments from different sequences once baseline order knowledge exists; keep early trials more blocked, then blend sequences as mastery rises.

## Chunking and Serial Order Constraints
- Learners naturally form chunks; stable chunks reduce load and anchor serial recall.
- Errors often reflect positional swaps or boundary confusions; cueing chunk boundaries and using windowed practice (adjacent steps) reduces these slips.
- Practical takeaways: practice overlapping windows (e.g., steps 3-6, then 5-8) to reinforce transitions; surface chunk labels/titles to stabilise anchors; keep step indices consistent and contiguous.

## Feedback and Errorfulness
- Immediate, informative feedback limits drift in serial order and accelerates correction of transition errors.
- Allowing some errorful retrieval (versus only errorless copying) tends to improve later recall if feedback is prompt; however, fragile learners may benefit from brief guided cues before full errors.
- Practical takeaways: let users attempt the next step, then show the target and positioning; treat near-misses as partially successful but still reveal the correct next step; avoid hiding feedback behind extra clicks.
